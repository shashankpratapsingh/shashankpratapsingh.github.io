Why Quivers Keep Showing Up

A short reflection on why directed graphs turn out to be the right bookkeeping device for representation theory, and what that bookkeeping starts to forget once we leave vector spaces behind.

Gabriel’s Theorem is one of those results that feels like a small miracle the first time you meet it: the indecomposable representations of a quiver are finite in number exactly when the underlying graph is a simply-laced Dynkin diagram, and they are counted by the positive roots. Combinatorics on one side, representation theory on the other, and a clean bijection in between.

The miracle is also a warning. It tells us that some of representation theory is really combinatorics in disguise. The question I keep returning to is: how much?

The categorical lift

When the ambient category is no longer vector spaces but a tensor category (say, the representation category of a Taft algebra), the naive notion of “quiver of an algebra” stops being obviously well defined. Arrows want to carry more data; the Hom-spaces are themselves objects with structure.

A good generalization should make the original theorem look like a shadow of something larger, not like a special case bolted on after the fact.

That is the test I hold the dissertation to.

What the picture forgets

Every invariant forgets something. The art is in choosing what to forget on purpose. More on that in a later note.
