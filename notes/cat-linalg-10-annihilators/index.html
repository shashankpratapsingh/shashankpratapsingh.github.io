Polynomial Relations and Annihilator Ideals

The equation p(T) = 0 is not a categorical statement. It is a shadow of something richer: a natural transformation from a polynomial expression to the zero functor. Passing to the Grothendieck group recovers the classical annihilator ideal.

In ordinary linear algebra, the minimal polynomial of a vector $v$ with respect to an operator $T$ is the monic polynomial $p$ of least degree such that $p(T)v = 0$. The equation $p(T)v = 0$ is a statement about an element of a vector space. Categorically, neither “element” nor “zero” transfers directly. The correct replacement involves natural transformations, and the classical ideal theory emerges only after passing to a decategorified shadow.

Polynomial relations as natural transformations

Definition. A polynomial relation on $S$ is a natural transformation

$$\rho : P(S) \Rightarrow 0,$$

where $0$ denotes the zero functor (sending every object to the zero object).

At each object $M$, the component $\rho_M : P(S)(M) \to 0$ is a map to the zero object.

In an abelian category, a morphism to the zero object is the zero morphism. So a polynomial relation at the level of objects says that the functor $P(S)$ sends every object to zero. But the natural transformation $\rho$ is the relation as structure, not merely as a truth value.

Warning. A polynomial expression $P(S)$ is not a relation. A polynomial expression is a functor: an object in $\operatorname{Fun}_k(\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{A})$. A relation is additional data: a natural transformation $P(S) \Rightarrow 0$. The two are not the same and should not be confused.

The Grothendieck group and the annihilator ideal

To recover ideal theory, we pass to the Grothendieck group. Assume $\mathcal{A}$ is an exact $k$-linear category.

Definition. The Grothendieck group $K_0(\mathcal{A})$ is the abelian group generated by isomorphism classes $[M]$ of objects, with the relation $[M] = [M'] + [M'']$ for every short exact sequence $0 \to M' \to M \to M'' \to 0$.

An endofunctor $S$ that is exact induces a group homomorphism $[S] : K_0(\mathcal{A}) \to K_0(\mathcal{A})$ by $[M] \mapsto [S(M)]$. Iteration gives $[S]^n([M]) = [S^n(M)]$. The group $K_0(\mathcal{A})$ becomes a $k[x]$-module by $x \cdot [M] = [S(M)]$.

Definition. For $[M] \in K_0(\mathcal{A})$, the annihilator ideal is

$$\operatorname{Ann}_{k[x]}([M]) = \{p(x) \in k[x] : p([S])[M] = 0 \text{ in } K_0(\mathcal{A})\}.$$

Proposition. The set $\operatorname{Ann}_{k[x]}([M])$ is an ideal in $k[x]$.

Proof. The zero polynomial satisfies $0 \cdot [M] = 0$ by definition of the module structure. If $p([S])[M] = 0$ and $q([S])[M] = 0$, then $(p + q)([S])[M] = p([S])[M] + q([S])[M] = 0$. If $p([S])[M] = 0$ and $r(x) \in k[x]$, then $(rp)([S])[M] = r([S]) \circ p([S])[M] = r([S])(0) = 0$. Both closure conditions hold, so $\operatorname{Ann}_{k[x]}([M])$ is an ideal. $\square$

If this ideal is nonzero, its monic generator is the minimal polynomial of $[M]$ with respect to $S$.

What $K_0$ forgets

The passage from $\mathcal{A}$ to $K_0(\mathcal{A})$ forgets morphisms and all non-split extensions. The annihilator ideal in $K_0(\mathcal{A})$ captures ideal-theoretic control over $[M]$, but it does not determine the categorical polynomial relations for $M$.

A categorical polynomial relation $P(S) \Rightarrow 0$ implies that $|P|([S])[M] = 0$ in $K_0(\mathcal{A})$, so $|P|(x) \in \operatorname{Ann}_{k[x]}([M])$. The converse fails in general: an element of the annihilator ideal need not lift to a categorical relation.

The $K_0$ annihilator is a shadow of the categorical theory, not a substitute for it.

The relation to linear factors

The classical minimal polynomial factors into linear factors $x - \mu$ over an algebraically closed field. Each linear factor corresponds to an eigenvalue. Categorically, a linear factor datum is exactly a natural transformation $\lambda : \operatorname{id} \Rightarrow S$. Higher-order factors correspond to generalized eigendata built from iterated application of $\lambda$.

This connection between polynomial relations, annihilator ideals, and linear factors is the subject of the next post.
