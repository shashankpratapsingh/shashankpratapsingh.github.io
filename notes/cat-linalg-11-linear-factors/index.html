Linear Factors, Generalized Eigenobjects, and Factorization

Eigenvalues are the degree-one part of polynomial theory. Linear factors and their powers produce generalized eigenobjects, and factorization of categorified polynomials carries strictly more information than factorization of their scalar shadows.

In ordinary linear algebra over an algebraically closed field, the minimal polynomial of an operator $T$ factors completely into linear factors $(x - \mu_i)^{n_i}$. The eigenvalue $\mu_i$ gives the degree-one part; the power $n_i$ encodes the Jordan block structure. This post identifies the categorical analogues of both.

Linear factor data

The natural transformation $\lambda : \operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{A}} \Rightarrow S$ is a linear factor datum.

The term is chosen deliberately. In the polynomial $x - \mu$, the scalar $\mu$ gives a specific map from the identity operator to $T$: it is the datum $v \mapsto \mu v$. The natural transformation $\lambda$ plays the same role: it gives a specific map from the identity functor to $S$, one component per object.

Canonical $\lambda$-eigenobjects, as defined in Post II, are the categorical degree-one part of the theory. They correspond to the case where the minimal polynomial has degree one.

Generalized eigenobjects

In linear algebra, a generalized eigenvector of order $n$ for $T$ with eigenvalue $\mu$ satisfies $(T - \mu \operatorname{id})^n v = 0$. The categorified analogue requires iterated application of the linear factor datum $\lambda$.

For $n = 1$, the eigenobject condition is $s_M = \lambda_M$.

For $n = 2$, one expects a condition involving two applications of the linear factor datum: the structure map $s_M$ and its behavior under $S(\lambda)$.

At this level of generality, the precise definition of a generalized $\lambda$-relation of rank $n$ depends on the ambient category and on how the defect between $s_M$ and $\lambda_M$ propagates under $S$. The organizing principle is general:

Generalized eigendata of rank $n$ come from $n$-fold iterated applications of the linear factor datum $\lambda$, with the discrepancy between consecutive steps recorded by additional structure maps.

In specific settings (such as exact or abelian categories with a reasonable notion of filtration), the generalized eigenobjects of rank $n$ form a subcategory of $\operatorname{Rep}(S)$ that contains $\operatorname{Eig}_\lambda(S)$ as a full subcategory.

Factorization data for categorified polynomials

An ordinary polynomial factorization $p(x) = q(x) r(x)$ is an equation in $k[x]$. Its categorical analogue is more structured.

Definition. A factorization datum for a categorified polynomial expression $P(S) = \bigoplus_i V_i \otimes S^i$ is a pair of categorified polynomial expressions $Q(S)$ and $R(S)$ together with a specified natural isomorphism

$$P(S) \xrightarrow{\sim} Q(S) \circ R(S).$$

Proposition. If $P(S) \cong Q(S) \circ R(S)$, then $|P|(x) = |Q|(x) \cdot |R|(x)$.

Proof. By the Cauchy product theorem of Post IX, the scalar shadow satisfies $|Q \circ R|(x) = |Q|(x) \cdot |R|(x)$, and the isomorphism preserves the scalar shadow. $\square$

The converse fails. A factorization $|P|(x) = |Q|(x) \cdot |R|(x)$ in $k[x]$ does not determine a factorization datum $P(S) \cong Q(S) \circ R(S)$.

The scalar shadow records only the dimensions of the coefficient spaces. A factorization datum requires the coefficient spaces themselves and a specific isomorphism of functors. Two categorified polynomials with the same scalar shadow can have genuinely different factorization properties.

Example. Let $k = \mathbb{R}$ and consider two categorified polynomials with scalar shadow $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$. One might have coefficient spaces $V_0 = \mathbb{R}^2$ and $V_2 = 0$ with a specific structure; another might have $V_0 = \mathbb{R}$, $V_1 = 0$, $V_2 = \mathbb{R}$ with different structure. Their scalar shadows agree, but their factorization data can differ.

The connection to eigenvalues

The factorization of the scalar minimal polynomial $p(x) = \prod_i (x - \mu_i)^{n_i}$ corresponds, categorically, to:

  • a linear factor datum $\lambda_i$ for each eigenvalue $\mu_i$;
  • generalized $\lambda_i$-relations of rank $n_i$;
  • a factorization datum for $P(S)$ into factors corresponding to each $\lambda_i$.

The scalar theory sees only the eigenvalues $\mu_i$ and their multiplicities $n_i$. The categorical theory sees the natural transformations $\lambda_i$ and the specific isomorphisms between the factor functors.
